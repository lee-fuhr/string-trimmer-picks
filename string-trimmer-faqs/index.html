Quick answer

This guide answers the most common questions about string trimmers, from choosing between gas and battery to understanding line thickness and cutting swath. We'll help you pick the right tool for your yard without the marketing noise.

Gas, battery, or corded: which is right for you?

Gas trimmers offer the most power and run time, making them a solid choice for large properties with heavy brush. But they require fuel mixing, regular maintenance, and produce noise and fumes that are hard to ignore. Battery models have improved dramatically. Modern lithium-ion packs can handle a typical suburban lot on a single charge, and they start instantly with far less noise. The trade-off is that run time drops as the battery ages, and you may need a second battery for bigger yards. Corded electric trimmers are the middle ground: unlimited run time, lightweight, and no battery anxiety. You’re tied to an outlet and an extension cord, so they work best for small, tidy lawns. Think about your yard size and how much hassle you’re willing to tolerate.

What size trimmer do I need for my yard?

Trimmer size isn’t just about engine or motor power, it’s about cutting swath, line thickness, and overall weight. A trimmer with a wider swath (the width of the cutting path) covers ground faster, but it’s usually heavier and harder to maneuver in tight spots. For a quarter-acre lot or less, a lightweight battery model with a moderate swath and thinner line (around 0.065 to 0.080 inches) typically does the job without tiring you out. For bigger yards or heavy weeds, step up to thicker line (0.095 inches or more) and a gas or high-voltage battery model with a wider swath. Don’t overlook balance: a well-balanced trimmer feels almost weightless in your hands. If you can, visit a store and hold a few models to see how they feel before you buy.

What does line diameter and cutting swath mean?

Line diameter is the thickness of the nylon string that spins to cut vegetation. Thicker line (like 0.095 or 0.105 inches) is more durable and can hack through tougher weeds, but it requires a more powerful trimmer and wears down batteries faster. Thinner line (0.065 to 0.080 inches) is gentler on grass and works well for light trimming around flower beds. Cutting swath is the total width of the line’s rotation, usually measured in inches. A wider swath means each pass cuts a bigger path, so you finish faster. But a very wide swath can be unwieldy in tight corners or near fences. Most residential trimmers offer a swath between 12 and 17 inches. Match the line to your primary task: thin line for light lawn edge, thick line for brambles and tall weeds. And remember that a larger swath usually means a heavier, more powerful machine.

Should I get a straight or curved shaft?

A straight shaft puts the cutting head directly in line with the motor, giving you better reach under shrubs and along edges. It’s common on gas trimmers and higher-end battery models, and it tends to offer more torque because the drive shaft is more efficient. Curved shaft trimmers are lighter and easier to maneuver, especially for shorter users. The curve brings the center of gravity closer to your body, reducing fatigue during long sessions. The trade-off is slightly less reach and often a less durable drive system. If you’re tall or have lots of obstacles to reach under, go straight. If you’re on the shorter side or just want a lighter tool for quick touch-ups, curved is a fine choice. Both work well for most trimming tasks.

How important is the bump feed head or other line advancement systems?

All string trimmers need a way to feed out fresh line as it wears down. The classic bump feed lets you tap the head on the ground to release more line. It’s simple and reliable, though you do have to stop and tap periodically. Some budget trimmers still use a manual feed that requires you to stop and pull line by hand, avoid those if you value convenience. Newer systems include automatic feed (the trimmer senses when line is short and advances it) and fixed-line heads where you pre-cut several lengths. Automatic feeds can save time, but they sometimes waste line. Fixed-line heads eliminate the bump mechanism entirely and are popular with pros for reliability. For most homeowners, a quality bump feed is just fine. Just make sure the head accepts the line diameter you’ll be using, and check how easy it is to reload.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a string trimmer to edge my lawn?

Yes, many string trimmers come with an adjustable head that tilts to a vertical position for edging along sidewalks and driveways. Some models also have a wheeled attachment that makes edging more consistent. If edging is a primary use, look for a trimmer with a rotating or pivoting head.

How often do I need to replace the trimmer line?

You don’t replace the entire line at once; you feed out new line as it wears. How often depends on what you’re cutting. Light grass may last several sessions; thick weeds or hitting a fence can wear it down in minutes. Most trimmers hold enough line for a few hours of typical use before you need to reload the spool.

Are battery trimmers powerful enough for thick weeds?

Yes, many modern battery trimmers handle thick weeds and light brush, especially models with high-voltage packs (36V and up) and thicker line diameters. But if you regularly battle heavy blackberry vines or saplings, a gas trimmer still has the edge in raw sustained power.

What does 'dual line' mean and do I need it?

Dual line trimmers have a head that holds two separate strands of line, spinning opposite directions for a wider, more aggressive cut. They cut faster and can handle thicker material, but they use more line and drain batteries quicker. For most residential trimming, a single line is sufficient.

Should I wear safety gear when trimming?

Absolutely. A string trimmer can kick up rocks, glass, and debris at high speed. At a minimum, wear safety glasses or goggles, long pants, closed-toe shoes, and hearing protection, especially with gas models. For heavy brush, a pair of chaps or heavy-duty pants adds an extra layer of protection.

In shortChoosing the right string trimmer comes down to your yard size, the kind of vegetation you tackle, and how much maintenance you’re willing to put up with. For most homeowners, a well-reviewed battery model with a comfortable grip and a decent bump feed head will cover all the basics without the hassle of gas. Take the time to match the line diameter and swath to your typical task, and you’ll end up with a tool that makes trimming feel like a quick chore rather than a full workout.
